# Trouble

## Beating a Gloom

A Gloom is not a health bar. It is a **hold** — the grip that a piece of
neglect has on something that used to be cared for. The clock measures how deep
the grip goes, and you win by prising it off one finger at a time.

**The clock starts full.** Four segments, six, or eight, and every one of them
filled in before the first roll. That is how bad it already is. Nobody let it
get this way on purpose; that is rather the point.

| Your result | The clock |
|---|---|
| **Miss** | Gloom **+1** if there is room. If it is already full, **you lose 1 Shine instead.** |
| **Mixed** | Gloom **−1**, and you pay a cost |
| **Hit** | Gloom **−1** |
| **Flourish** | Gloom **−2** |

**Empty it and you win.** The shape comes apart, and back in the everyday the
thing it was holding comes loose: the route runs, the door opens, the person
picks up the phone.

**A Miss is never free.** Early in a Number the clock is already at maximum, so
a Miss has nowhere to add its tick — and a rule that did nothing there would
make missing *cheaper* than succeeding, since a Mixed always costs something.
So instead the Gloom pushes back on you. It has nowhere left to spread, so it
comes for your Shine.

**The Gloom never gets worse than it started.** A Miss's tick can restore lost
ground, but it can never take the clock past its starting size. The Gloom does
not escalate, plot, or adapt. It endures. That is what makes it Gloom rather
than a villain, and it is why the fight is always winnable and never quick.

### The last tick belongs to the climax

**A Gloom clock cannot be taken below 1 before the Big Finish.**

One sentence, and it does a lot of work. However well the Verse and Chorus go —
however hot the dice run, however clever the plan — the killing blow waits for
the beat that was built for it. A team who tear through the early scenes arrive
at the Big Finish with the clock on one and every advantage; they simply do not
get to end the story in its second scene.

The Showrunner should say this out loud the first time it comes up, rather than
fudging a roll: *"That would take it to zero, so it stops at one — and it is
visibly barely holding. Save it."*

### Losing

The Gloom wins **only if every Star is Dimmed at once.**

Nobody dies. Nobody is maimed, captured, or written out. The team simply cannot
go on tonight. You come back to the everyday, and the thing you went for stays
grey, and somebody has to come back for it another time.

Let that be quiet when it happens. Do not stage a defeat cinematic. The right
note is five people on a pavement at two in the morning, and one of them saying
*"we'll come back."*

**If one result does both** — a last Star on 1 Shine rolls a Mixed against a
clock on 1, empties it, and the cost Dims them — **the team wins.** Emptying is
checked first. The Star goes down *as* the thing falls, which is the best image
this genre has, and they are still Dimmed when the Encore opens, and somebody
still has to reach them.

## Gloom clocks: size and setup

Three sizes. Pick by how much of the everyday is involved.

| Size | Scope | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | A bad afternoon | One person forgotten for a week |
| **6** | A real problem | A cancelled bus route; a library cut to two days |
| **8** | A season's antagonist | A community centre being wound down by committee |

For a first Number use a **6**. Four resolves before the Bridge has landed;
eight is a long night unless the table already knows the rules.

Building one is Chapter 7's job, but the question that sizes it is short:
**what does this neglect cost, and who pays?** If one person pays, it is a 4.
If a street pays, it is a 6. If it has a budget line and a review date, it is
an 8.

## Getting Dimmed

At **0 Shine** you are **Dimmed**.

You are still there. That matters. You are not unconscious and not removed —
you are *greyed*, standing in the middle of the Spectrum with all the colour
gone out of you, able to see everything and do nothing. Your Radiance drops.
Your phrase will not come.

You cannot act, cannot roll, cannot back anyone, and — importantly — **cannot
morph**. There is no spending your way out of this. One Sparkle does not buy a
rescue.

Play it honestly. If your Star is Dimmed, say less. Let the silence sit. The
table will notice, which is the mechanism working.

## Reaching someone

This is the best rule in the book, so it gets said plainly.

**Any Star can reach a Dimmed Star. It always works. There is no roll.**

The rescuer describes it — a scene, a truth, a hand held out, a name said
properly for the first time all night. Then:

- It costs the rescuer **1 Shine or 1 Sparkle**, their choice.
- **In the Big Finish**, it also costs their action for that round.
- **Outside the Big Finish**, where there are no rounds, it costs them the
  scene: they spend it going after the person instead of doing anything else.
- The Dimmed Star comes back with **2 Shine**.
- **Both** notes sound on the Chord — the rescuer's for reaching, the rescued
  Star's for being reached.

No roll, ever. Not a hard one, not an easy one. In a game whose entire argument
is that people can reach each other, putting dice on the reaching would be
saying the opposite.

What the rescuer says matters more than what they spend. The Showrunner should
ask for it: *"What do you say to her?"* — and then wait.

## Chases, arguments, and other trouble

Everything uses the same roll. Only the fiction changes.

**A chase** is Flash if you are running, Craft if you are cutting the corner
they will not think of, Cool if you are simply refusing to hurry. Difficulty by
how far ahead they are.

**An argument** is Heart if you are being honest, Cool if you are being
composed, Craft if you are being right. Mixed results in arguments should
almost always cost **Exposure**, because that is what arguments do.

**A crowd** is Flash to move through, Heart to move, Cool to be believed by.

**A locked thing** is Craft, unless somebody has a more interesting idea, in
which case it is that.

The Showrunner does not need a subsystem for any of this. Name a Difficulty,
call for the Trait that matches how they are doing it, and apply the ladder.
PRISM has exactly one resolution mechanic and it is load-bearing everywhere.

## The Encore scene

Between Numbers, the lights come up. The Encore is short, quiet, and not
optional.

Go round the table. Each Star gets one scene, or one exchange, in the everyday —
a kitchen, a bus stop, a doorstep, a stairwell at four in the morning. No dice.
No Gloom. Just people who did something enormous together, being ordinary near
each other.

Mechanically, the Encore does four things:

- **Shine refills completely**, for everyone, including anyone who ended Dimmed.
- **Sparkle refills completely**, up to your maximum.
- **Spent Bonds come back** — and every returning Bond gets **rewritten**,
  because something different is true now. This is the most important part.
  Read the old line, then write the new one, out loud.
- **The Chord clears.** Next Number, everybody starts silent again.

And two things that are not mechanical but matter more: check in on lines and
veils, and ask each player what they loved. Thirty seconds each.

## If you run a second Number

Here is the whole of advancement in PRISM. It is deliberately not a chapter.

**Your Traits never change.** Not ever. There is no experience, no levelling,
no way to buy your +0 up to a +1. A Star who has saved a town four times has
exactly the same numbers as one who has never left the bus stop.

That is genre fidelity, not laziness. The shows this game is made of do not
have characters who get statistically stronger; they have characters who get
*better known*. So what changes is everything that is not a number:

- **Bonds rewrite** at every Encore, as above.
- **Radiance evolves.** New colour in the palette, a new sound, a line added to
  the phrase. Free, unbounded, whenever it feels true.
- **Your Refrain may be rewritten** — but only once it has genuinely been
  tested. When a Star has held to their line at real cost, or broken it and had
  to live with that, they may write a new one. A rewritten Refrain is the
  clearest statement this game has that somebody grew.

There is exactly one number that moves. **Your maximum Sparkle rises by 1 after
every second Number, to a hard cap of 5.** It is the only growth in the game,
and it was chosen precisely because it touches no Difficulty, no ceiling, and
none of the probabilities in Chapter 2.

If your group wants more than that, you do not want more rules. You want
another Number. Go and pick a different thing in your town that somebody
stopped caring about.

## A Big Finish, end to end

The rules above, running once.

> **The clock is on 3.** The Nightline are transformed and it is round two.
> Wren is on 2 Shine, Toby on 4, Priya on 1, Marisol on 5, Danny on 3.
>
> **Wren** goes first. She wants to get between the thing and the last person
> on the pavement — Heart, and the Showrunner calls it **Tricky**. Danny backs
> her and pays 1 Shine (Danny to 2). Three dice: 5, 2, 6. Keep 6 and 5 — that's
> 11, plus Heart +2 is 13. Beats 9 by four. **Hit.** Clock to **2**.
>
> **Toby** goes. He wants to get *under* it, which is Flash and, because it is
> loud and public and first, his Signature drops it a band: Dazzling becomes
> **Tricky**. He rolls 3 and 3 — 6, plus Flash +2 is 8. **Miss.**
>
> The clock is not full, so it ticks back up. Clock to **3**. The Showrunner
> describes the doors grinding open again.
>
> **Priya** is on 1 Shine and goes anyway. Craft, Tricky. She rolls 4 and 4 —
> 8, plus Craft +2 is 10. Beats 9 by one: **Mixed**. Clock to **2**, and the
> Showrunner names the cost: **Shine**. Priya to 0.
>
> **Priya is Dimmed.** She stands there in the middle of it with the yellow
> gone out of her.
>
> **Marisol** spends her whole action reaching her. It costs her 1 Shine
> (Marisol to 4). No roll — it simply works. She says: *"Breathe. I've got the
> door."* Priya comes back with **2 Shine**.
>
> And both notes sound. Marisol's for reaching, Priya's for being reached.
> **The Chord is full.**
>
> **Danny**, last in the round, looks at the clock on 2 and at four people who
> are all still standing, and says: *"Together?"*

Everything in this chapter is in that exchange: a full clock ticking back up on
a Miss, a Mixed that succeeded and hurt, a Dimming that was not a death, a
rescue with no dice on it, and two notes sounding at once because somebody went
and got their friend.

## What the Showrunner is doing during all this

Three things, and no more.

**Name the Difficulty before the dice.** Out loud, and then do not move it. If a
player argues for a lower one, listen before they roll and not after.

**Make a move on every Miss.** The default is the clock. When the clock is full
and it comes off the Star's Shine instead, describe *why* — the Gloom turning
its attention, the cold coming off it, the sheer weight of being near a thing
that does not care.

**Ask for the words.** On a rescue, on a morph, on a Refrain that just cost
somebody something: *"What do you say?"* Then wait. The waiting is the job.

You are not adjudicating a combat. There is one mechanic, it fits on a card,
and your actual role is to keep asking people what they said out loud.
