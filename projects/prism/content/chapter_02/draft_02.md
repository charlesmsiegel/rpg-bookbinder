# How to Play

## The roll: 2d6 + Trait

When something is genuinely uncertain and it matters how it goes, you roll.

Take **two six-sided dice**. Roll them. Add them together. Add the **Trait**
that fits what you are doing. Compare the total to a **Difficulty** the
Showrunner names before you pick up the dice.

That is the entire resolution system. Everything else in this book is
decoration on that sentence.

You have four Traits, and each of them is rated **+0**, **+1**, or **+2**:

- **Heart**: sincerity, courage, connection. Standing your ground. Meaning it.
- **Flash**: speed, grace, performance. Physical daring, and doing it with style.
- **Craft**: cleverness, making, fixing, knowing. Taking a thing apart.
- **Cool**: composure, style, reading a room. Being the one who does not panic.

Every Star has one Trait at +2, two at +1, and one at +0. That never changes;
there is no advancement in PRISM and no way to buy your +0 up. The +0 is not a
flaw to be fixed. It is the most interesting thing on your sheet, and Chapter 3
explains why at length.

**Which Trait?** The one that matches *how* you are doing it, not *what* you
are doing. Getting past a locked door with Craft means picking it. With Flash
it means going through the window. With Heart it means knocking, and meaning
it. With Cool it means walking in like you were expected. The Showrunner
should say yes to any of these if you can describe it convincingly.

Roll only when failure would be interesting. If your Star wants to get a cat
out of a tree and nothing is at stake, they get the cat out of the tree.

## Difficulties: 6, 9, 11

Three numbers. Learn them once and never look them up again.

| Difficulty | Name | Number | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | Anyone could | **6** | Awkward, not hard. You would manage on a bad day. |
| Tricky | Takes something from you | **9** | The honest middle. Most rolls live here. |
| Dazzling | Should not be possible | **11** | You are asking the world to make an exception. |

Dazzling is *hard*. With a +2 Trait, two dice cap out at 14, which is exactly
what a clean success at Dazzling requires: you need both dice to come up six
and your best Trait to be the right one. That is intentional. Most of the time
you do the impossible thing and it costs you something, and that is the correct
feeling.

The Showrunner picks the Difficulty before the roll, out loud, and does not
change it afterwards. If a player has an argument for a lower one, they should
make it before the dice hit the table.

## The outcome ladder

Compare your total to the Difficulty.

| Result | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **Under it** | **Miss.** It doesn't work, and the Showrunner makes a move. |
| **Meets it, or beats it by 1–2** | **Mixed.** It works, and it costs you. |
| **Beats it by 3 or more** | **Hit.** Clean. You get what you wanted. |
| **Both kept dice show 6**, on any success | **Flourish.** Clean, twice as effective, plus something extra. |

A **Miss** never means "nothing happens". The Showrunner always changes the
situation: that is the deal. The scene moves, just not the way you wanted.

A **Mixed** is a success. Say that part out loud at your table, because tables
who have played other games sometimes hear "mixed" and mentally file it under
failure. You did the thing. You just paid for it, and the next section says
exactly what with.

A **Flourish** is double sixes on the two dice you keep, on any roll that
succeeded. Not a margin, sixes. It is the same trigger at Easy and at
Dazzling, which is why it never becomes unreachable at the top of the ladder.

And here is the part that will matter more than you expect: **because you always
keep the best two dice, a Flourish gets more likely the more friends are
helping you.**

| Backers | Dice you roll | Chance of a Flourish |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 2d6 | 2.78% |
| 1 | 3d6 | 7.41% |
| 2 | 4d6 | 13.19% |
| 3 | 5d6 | 19.62% |

On your own, a Flourish is a once-a-night lightning strike. With the whole team
behind you it is a one-in-five shot. Friendship in PRISM does not just make you
more reliable. It makes you *spectacular*.

## What a Mixed result costs

You succeeded. Now pay.

The Showrunner names **one** cost from this list. You may always refuse the one
they picked and lose **1 Shine** instead, that option is always open, so no
Mixed result can ever stall the table arguing about it.

| Cost | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Shine** | Lose 1. You are more worn down than you were. |
| **Sparkle** | Spend 1. Cannot be chosen if you have none left. |
| **Exposure** | Something true comes out, a secret, a position, a feeling. No number; the fiction changes. |
| **Time** | The Gloom gains 1 tick. You were busy, and it got a beat. |

Pick the one that hurts in the way the fiction wants. If a Star talks their way
past a security guard on a Mixed, Exposure is lovely, the guard now knows
their face. If they are running a rooftop, Shine. If they are arguing with
someone they love, Exposure again, and let it land.

**Exposure has no number on purpose.** It is the cost that changes the story
rather than the sheet, and it is usually the best one.

## Sparkle

**Sparkle** is your personal fuel. You start each Number with **3** and can
never hold more than **5**.

Spend 1 Sparkle to:

- **Morph**: a Solo Morph, which Chapter 4 covers properly.
- **Reroll one die.** Any one die in the pool, once, after seeing it. If friends
  added dice, you may reroll any of them, including one you were not going to
  keep. Then keep the best two as normal.
- **Ignore 1 Shine loss.** You shrug it off. It looked like it hurt; it didn't.
- **Fuel a Finisher**: doubling the damage you do to a Gloom, once you are
  transformed.

You get Sparkle back by being who you said you were. Every Star has a
**Refrain**, a one-line creed. **When your Refrain costs you something in a
scene, take 1 Sparkle back**, once per scene, never above your maximum.

That is the loop the whole economy runs on. Your creed is not decoration; it is
your income. A Star whose Refrain is *"I don't leave people in the dark"* and
who keeps walking past people in the dark will run out of Sparkle, and should.

At an **Encore**, the downtime scene between Numbers, Sparkle refills
completely.

Sparkle is the thing you spend on yourself. It is deliberately *not* the
friendship mechanic. That one is next, and it works differently on purpose.

## Power of Friendship

This is the centre of the game.

When another Star rolls, you may **back them**. Say how: what you shout, what
you do, what you put at risk. Then add **one extra d6** to their roll.

**They still keep only two dice.** Roll the whole handful, keep the best two,
add the Trait. Every die a friend adds is another chance at a good pair, but
the total never gets bigger than a two-dice total. Backing makes a good result
*likelier*, never larger. All the numbers in this book are calibrated for two
dice and stay that way no matter how many people pile in.

Here is what backing is worth, at Tricky with a +1 Trait:

| Backers | Chance of success |
|---|---|
| 0 | 41.7% |
| 1 | 68.1% |
| 2 | 82.6% |
| 3 | 90.6% |

One friend turns a coin flip into a comfortable favourite. That is not a
rounding error; that is the difference between the story going your way and
not.

### Backing costs the backer

Always. Pick one when you back:

- **Lose 1 Shine.** You put yourself in it.
- **Spend 1 Sparkle.** You burn something you were saving.
- **Give up your next action**: only during a Big Finish, where turns exist
  and that means something. In an ordinary scene, pick a different cost.
- **Expose something.** A secret, a weakness, a feeling you would rather not
  say out loud. This is usually the good one.

If you have an unspent **Bond** with the Star you are backing, you may **spend
the Bond** instead of paying anything else. It is the cheapest way to help
someone, and it is finite: a spent Bond stays spent until the next Encore,
where it comes back changed. You cannot lean on the same friendship all night.

### One roll, all the way through

Here is the whole system happening once, so you can see the shape of it.

> **Showrunner:** The bus is pulling away from the stop and it is enormous —
> three storeys of chrome and rust, and the doors are closing on the last
> person still on the pavement. What do you do?
>
> **Priya's player:** I want to jam the doors. Not with my hands: there's a
> hydraulic line running down the frame and I want to crimp it.
>
> **Showrunner:** Lovely. That's **Craft**, and it's **Tricky**. Difficulty 9.
> You're reaching into a moving machine the size of a house.
>
> **Priya's player:** Craft +2. Rolling two dice.
>
> *She rolls a 4 and a 3. Total 7, plus 2, is 9.*
>
> **Showrunner:** That meets it. Nine exactly, so it's a **Mixed**, the line
> crimps, the doors judder and stop. And it costs you. I'm going to say
> **Exposure**: your arm is inside the frame up to the shoulder and everyone on
> that pavement can see exactly who is doing this. You are not anonymous
> anymore.
>
> **Priya's player:** Ugh. Yes. Fine.

Now run the same moment with a friend in it.

> **Toby's player:** Can I back her? I skid in low and put my board under the
> door so it can't finish closing.
>
> **Showrunner:** You can. What does it cost you?
>
> **Toby's player:** Exposure as well — I'll shout her name. Loudly. Everyone
> hears it.
>
> **Showrunner:** Then Priya rolls **three** dice and keeps the best two.
>
> *She rolls 4, 3, and 6. She keeps the 6 and the 4. Total 10, plus 2, is 12.*
>
> **Showrunner:** Twelve against nine: you beat it by three. That's a **Hit**.
> Clean. The doors stop dead, no cost at all, and the Gloom clock goes down one.

Same dice. Same Star. One friend, one shouted name, and a Mixed became a Hit.

Notice what Toby did *not* do: he did not make the roll bigger. Priya still
added two dice and one Trait. He gave her another chance at a good pair, and
paid for it himself.

### The two rules that make it mean something

**You cannot back yourself.** Obviously.

**You cannot back someone you have never been honest with.** If your Stars have
not had a real conversation, if nothing true has passed between them this
Number, the help is not available yet. Go and have the conversation. The
Showrunner should enforce this warmly, as an invitation rather than a penalty:
*"You'd love to help her, and you realise you don't actually know what's wrong.
Ask her."*

That rule is the whole game compressed into one sentence. In PRISM you do not
get stronger by acquiring things. You get stronger by being known.

**If you are using the ready-made Stars in Chapter 6**, their Bonds are already
written, so they can all back each other from the first scene. That is
deliberate: the rule exists for Stars a table builds itself, where the honesty
has to happen at the table before the help is available. One pair in Chapter 6
has been left deliberately unfinished so you can see the rule bite.

## Shine and Gloom

Two tracks sit on the table, and the Big Finish is a race between them.

**Shine** is yours. Five boxes, though morphing lifts that: see Chapter 4. You lose Shine to Mixed costs, to backing your
friends, to a Gloom hitting back, and to pushing when you should not have.

At **0 Shine** you are **Dimmed**. You are not dead, nothing in PRISM kills
you. You are out of the scene, greyed out, still there, still visible, unable
to act. It is a horrible feeling and it is meant to be, because the way out of
it is the best rule in the book: **someone can always reach you.** No roll. It
always works. Chapter 5 has the details.

**Gloom** is the monster's. It is a clock of 4, 6, or 8 segments, and it
**starts full**: that is how deep the neglect goes. You win by emptying it.

| Your result | The clock |
|---|---|
| Miss | Gloom **+1**, if there is room. If it is already full, **you lose 1 Shine instead**, it has nowhere to spread, so it pushes back on you. |
| Mixed | Gloom **−1**, and you pay a cost |
| Hit | Gloom **−1** |
| Flourish | Gloom **−2** |

Empty the clock and you win: the monster breaks and the thing it was holding
comes back to the everyday. The Gloom only wins if **every** Star is Dimmed at
once, and even then nobody dies. You just cannot go on tonight, and that place
stays grey until somebody comes back for it.

A Miss can never make a problem worse than it already was. The Gloom does not
grow. It endures. That is what makes it Gloom and not a villain.

Put both tracks where the whole table can see them, the clock in the middle,
everyone's Shine in front of them. The tension in a Big Finish is entirely in
those two numbers moving toward each other, and it is much better felt than
described. Watching four Stars burn Shine to back each other while the clock
crawls down is the game working exactly as designed.
