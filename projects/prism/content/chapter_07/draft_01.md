# Your Town and the Spectrum

## The everyday

The setting of PRISM is the place you actually live.

Not a version of it. Not a lightly fictionalised one with the street names
changed. Your town, with its actual bus routes and its actual weather and the
shop that has been "closing down" for four years.

This is a deliberate refusal. PRISM ships no world map, no gazetteer, no
timeline of Great Events, and no canon you could contradict. What it ships
instead is a **procedure** — a way of looking at where you already are until
you find the thing worth fighting for. That procedure is this chapter, and it
is most of what the Showrunner actually needs.

The everyday is not drab. Be careful of that. It is not a grey world that the
Spectrum redeems. It is simply the world with all its colour still folded up
inside it — the way white light carries every colour at once and shows you
none of them. Nothing is missing here. It is all present and unsplit.

There is no masquerade. Nobody is hiding anything from the general public,
because there is no secret to keep: the Spectrum is not classified, it is just
not somewhere most people have thought to step. Your Stars have ordinary lives,
ordinary names, and ordinary jobs, and nobody will section them for saying
where they were last night.

## The Spectrum

One turn to the left, and the light comes apart.

The Spectrum is the same geography rendered in howling airbrushed rainbow. The
same launderette on the same corner — but the sign is nine colours now, two of
which do not have names, and the letters have a chrome bevel that catches light
from a sun that is not in the sky.

Some things that are reliably true over there:

**Gradients happen to everything.** Skies, walls, water, the backs of hands.
Nothing is one colour. Nothing is ever one colour.

**Chrome and neon are structural.** Not decoration — load-bearing. Railings,
bus shelters and lamp posts are all mirror-finished and all reflecting a
slightly different scene than the one in front of them.

**Impossible animals are ordinary.** Dolphins moving through the air above the
ring road. A snow leopard on a roof, in space, somehow, without the roof
stopping being a roof. They do not react to you. They are not a portent. They
live here.

**Glitter behaves like weather.** It drifts, settles, banks up in doorways, and
is warm to the touch. It will be in your Stars' pockets for days.

**Sound arrives half a second late.** This is the single most useful trick for
running the place: after somebody says their phrase, there is a beat of total
silence before the world catches up. Use that beat.

And crucially — **it is the same place.** Everything a Star knows about the
everyday version still holds. The route still runs where it runs. The library
still has the broken window round the back. Winning here changes things there,
because there is no *there*: it is one location seen two ways.

## What the Gloom is

Something in your town has stopped being cared for.

Not attacked. Not cursed. Just — allowed to slide. A service quietly reduced. A
building nobody has the budget for. A person nobody has rung. The specific,
ordinary, entirely human process by which good things get dimmer because
looking after them was somebody's job and then it was nobody's.

In the everyday, that looks like a form, or a shrug, or a notice on a door.

In the Spectrum, it has a body.

**The Gloom does not want anything.** This is the most important sentence in
the chapter. It has no plan, no goal, no ideology, and nothing to negotiate
with. It is not evil and it is not even hostile in the way a villain is
hostile. It simply keeps taking, the way a low place keeps collecting water,
because that is the shape it has.

That is the horror of it — and it is also exactly why you can beat it. A thing
with no plan cannot adapt to yours. It endures; it does not escalate. Five
people who have decided to care about something, on purpose, at cost, are the
one thing it has no answer for.

## Turning a real problem into a Gloom

Five steps. This is the chapter's payload, and once you have it you never need
this book to invent an antagonist again.

### 1. Name a real local problem

Small and specific. Resist the urge to go large.

**Good:** the night bus that stopped running. The library open two days a week.
The pool that closed. The friend nobody has checked on. The stretch of river
that nobody clears.

**Bad:** urban decay. Austerity. Loneliness. These are true and they are
unfightable, and a monster made of them will be a fog rather than a thing.

The test: **can a Star point at it?** If they can walk to it and put a hand on
it, you have a Gloom. If they can only gesture at it, go smaller.

### 2. Ask what it costs, and who pays

This is the Gloom's diet, and it decides everything downstream.

The night bus does not cost money. It costs a particular kind of evening — the
one where you stay out because you can get home. It is paid for by the people
who now do not go, and who do not entirely notice they have stopped.

Write one sentence: *"This costs ______, and ______ pays it."* You will use it
in the Big Finish.

### 3. Build the Spectrum body

Now make it a thing.

Take the everyday form and ask what shape that neglect would have if it were
enormous and patient. The bus that stopped running becomes a bus the length of
a street, chrome and rust, headlights like dead suns, and — this is the part
that makes it a Gloom rather than a monster — **always pulling away.** Never
arriving. Never chasing. Just leaving, forever, with the doors closing.

Three rules for the body:

- **It should be recognisable.** Somebody should be able to guess the everyday
  problem from the Spectrum shape.
- **It should not be trying to hurt anyone.** It hurts people the way weather
  does.
- **It should be doing one thing forever.** Pulling away. Filling up. Getting
  quieter. Offering to do it for you.

### 4. Size the clock

**4** if one person pays. **6** if a street pays. **8** if it has a budget line
and a review date.

Then write three **moves** — the things it does on a Miss. Not attacks. Things
it *does*, which happen to cost you.

> *Strand someone. Make the distance longer. Convince them it was always like
> this.*

### 5. Decide what comes back

Finish before you start. Write the one concrete, small, specific thing that is
different in the everyday when the clock empties.

Not "hope returns to the neighbourhood". **The route runs again on Fridays, and
two people who had stopped seeing each other are on it.**

That sentence is the reward for the whole Number, and the Stars should hear it
in the last thirty seconds of the session, and it should be worth the evening.

## Four worked examples

Models, not canon. Steal the shape and change the details to your own town.

**The Quiet Shelf** — *clock 4.* A library cut from five days to two. In the
Spectrum: shelves that get taller and further apart as you walk between them,
and a returns slot that is always just out of reach. It costs a place to be
warm in for free; the people who have nowhere else pay. When it falls: the
Tuesday reading group starts again, four people, one of them new.

**The Silt** — *clock 6.* A stretch of river nobody clears. In the Spectrum: a
slow chrome tide, mirror-bright and thickening, swallowing landmarks one at a
time and reflecting them back slightly wrong. It costs the shape of the place
itself; everyone pays a little. When it falls: you can see the bottom by the
footbridge again.

**The Long Weekend** — *clock 4.* Someone nobody has rung in a month. In the
Spectrum: a very comfortable room that keeps adding doors, all of which lead
back into it, and a voice that keeps agreeing that you are probably too tired.
It costs one specific person; that person pays all of it. When it falls: they
answer, and they are embarrassed, and they come.

**The Grey Ledger** — *clock 8.* A community centre being wound down by
committee. In the Spectrum: an enormous filing structure that reclassifies
whatever it touches — a football pitch becomes an asset, a choir becomes a
line item, a person becomes a case. It costs everything at once, slowly. When
it falls: the booking system reopens and somebody books the hall for a party.

## Your town is the real setting

Those four are scaffolding. Take them down.

The best PRISM Number your table will ever play will be about something one of
you is genuinely annoyed about — a thing you walk past, a thing that closed,
somebody you have been meaning to ring. Ask at the start of a session: *"what's
been allowed to go grey near you?"* and then build the monster out of the
answer.

One caution, and it belongs here rather than in the safety chapter because it
is a craft note as much as a care note: **ask before you build a Gloom from
something close to somebody's actual life.** A hospital one, a benefits one, a
someone-in-this-room-is-burning-out one. Thirty seconds of asking buys you the
whole evening.

Then go and win it back.

## Running a scene in the Spectrum

Some practical notes, because describing a place this loud gets tiring fast.

**Name three things and stop.** One colour, one impossible object, one sound.
Not a paragraph. *"The launderette sign in nine colours, a snow leopard asleep
on the roof, and the hum arriving a half-second after you see the light."* The
players will build the rest and theirs will be better.

**Let the Stars add to it.** Ask directly: *"What's over there that shouldn't
be?"* Anything a player invents is now true. This is the fastest way to make
the Spectrum feel inhabited rather than performed, and it costs you nothing.

**Keep one everyday thing in frame.** A bin. A bus stop. A specific broken
paving slab. The Spectrum is disorienting precisely in proportion to how much
of the ordinary world is still recognisably in it, and a scene with no anchor
becomes a light show.

**Never explain it.** Nobody in the fiction knows why any of this works, the
Stars least of all. There is no lore to uncover, no origin, and no one to ask.
If a player theorises, let them be right for the evening and do not confirm it.

## The rule about coming back

Whatever happens in the Spectrum, the Stars come home.

Not as a mercy — as a structure. Every Number ends in the everyday, on the same
street, at roughly the same time of night, with the ordinary world very
slightly changed. That return is where the reward lands, and skipping it to end
on the big fight is the single most common way to waste a good session.

Give it thirty seconds. Describe the ordinary thing that is now different. Let
somebody notice glitter in a coat seam. Then stop.
