# Running the Game

## What the Showrunner does

You do three things.

**You describe the situation.** The everyday first, honestly and specifically,
and then the Spectrum when they cross. You are not a narrator of plot; you are
the person who says what it looks like and then shuts up.

**You name the Difficulty, before the dice.** Out loud, once, and then you do
not move it. If a player wants to argue for a lower one, they get to do that
before they roll and not after.

**You ask for the words.** This is the job. When a Star morphs: *"What do you
say?"* When someone reaches a Dimmed friend: *"What do you say to her?"* When a
Refrain has just cost somebody something: *"Say it to him."* Then you wait. The
waiting is not dead air. The waiting is the game.

And two things you never do:

**You never kill a Star.** Not as a twist, not as a consequence, not with
permission. The floor of this game is that people go dim and get reached, and
the moment that floor is not solid, players stop investing in the sincerity the
whole thing runs on.

**You never refuse a rescue.** Reaching a Dimmed Star always works. There is no
circumstance, no special Gloom, and no dramatic moment that makes it fail.

## Building a Number

A Number is one session and one complete story. Here is the prep, and it should
take about fifteen minutes.

**1. Pick the thing.** [Chapter 7](#your-town-and-the-spectrum)'s procedure. A real local problem somebody can
point at. If your players have been playing a while, ask *them* at the end of
the previous session and prep the one they name.

**2. Build the Gloom.** Everyday form, Spectrum body, three moves, clock size,
and the one concrete thing that comes back.

**3. Find the Bridge.** This is the only genuinely hard bit of prep, and it is
worth the time: **what is the truth that has to get said?**

Every Number has one. Somebody has to admit something, or ask for something, or
stop pretending. You do not need to know who says it, the table will decide
that, but you need to know roughly what it is, because that is the moment the
whole shape hinges on.

The hinge for each Gloom in [Chapter 9](#the-gloom):

| Gloom | The truth that has to get said |
|---|---|
| The Last Bus | *"I stopped going out and I didn't notice."* |
| The Quiet Shelf | *"That was the only warm place I had."* |
| The Silt | *"I walk past it every day. I've never once stopped."* |
| The Long Weekend | *"I assumed someone else had rung."* |
| The Grey Ledger | *"I filled in the form because it was easier than arguing."* |
| The Understudy | *"I think you'd all be fine without me."* |

Notice that every one is an admission rather than a discovery. The Bridge is
never a clue the Stars find; it is a thing one of them stops not saying.

**4. Write five hooks.** One per Star, straight off their sheets, a place
their Refrain gets tested, a thing their Signature is for, a Bond that could
come due. One line each. You will use three of them.

**5. Do not plan the middle.** You have an opening, a monster and a hinge. The
middle is theirs.

## The four beats in practice

### Verse, the everyday

Open in the ordinary world and stay there longer than feels comfortable.

This is the beat new Showrunners rush and it is the one that makes the rest
work. Spend real time on a kitchen, a shift, a bus stop, a group chat. Let the
Stars be people with lives. Ask small questions: what are you eating, who
texted you, what did you not do today that you meant to.

The problem arrives at the *end* of the Verse, and ideally it arrives as
something small and wrong rather than an attack. The bus does not come. The
door is locked when it should not be. Somebody does not reply.

**If the Verse runs long:** good. Let it. Cut in with the problem when the
energy dips, not on a schedule.

**If the Verse runs short:** the players are keen for action and you should give
it to them, but ask one question of each Star before you do.

### Chorus, it goes badly

First contact. They engage, and it does not work, and the clock is revealed for
what it is: **already full.**

The Chorus exists to establish that this cannot be solved by ordinary
competence. Let them try the sensible thing. Let the sensible thing be
insufficient. Nobody should morph here, or if somebody does, that is the
Bridge arriving early and you should treat it as such.

**If they are winning too easily:** remember the clock cannot go below 1 before
the Big Finish, and say so out loud rather than fudging.

### Bridge, the key change

Somebody says the thing. Then everybody morphs.

You cannot script this. What you can do is *make room for it*: cut away from
the action, put two Stars alone somewhere, and ask the question you wrote in
prep. *"You've got about thirty seconds before it comes back. What do you tell
her?"*

Then the command word, and the airtime rule, and no dice at all until it is
finished.

### Big Finish (transformed)
Turn order starts here and only here. Go round the table, one action each per
round, Showrunner acting between rounds.

Track two numbers where everyone can see them: the clock, and everybody's
Shine. That is the whole tension. Say the clock out loud every time it moves.

Watch the Chord. When it fills, do not announce it, let a player notice. They
will, and the moment somebody says *"…wait, are we all…?"* is the best moment
in the game.

## Making the Bridge land on time

The commonest way a PRISM session goes wrong is the Bridge never arriving. The
group stays in problem-solving mode, nobody says anything true, and you end up
in a long Chorus that gradually stops being fun.

Four tools:

**Separate them.** Two Stars alone in a stairwell will say things five Stars in
a fight will not.

**Ask directly.** You are allowed. *"Marisol, you've said 'I've got it' four
times tonight. Is that true?"*

**Make the ordinary approach fail once more.** Not punitively, just enough that
someone says *"we can't do this the normal way."*

**Have an NPC say a smaller version of it.** Somebody the Stars are helping,
admitting something minor. It gives the table permission.

And if a player morphs early? It depends on whether there is anything to morph
*at* yet.

**Once the problem is on the table**, which is the end of the Verse at the
latest, **that morph is the Bridge.** Do not block it. Roll with it: the key
change came early, so move the Number toward its Big Finish from there. If no
Gloom clock is running yet, the morph starts one, a transformation is itself a
declaration that something is wrong.

**Before the problem arrives**, in the ordinary-life opening, it is not a
transformation at all, just a flourish of light. It costs nothing, grants
nothing, and does not count as having morphed. Let them show off, and do not
start a clock for it.

## Showrunner moves

On a Miss, you make a move. **The Gloom's own three moves come first**: every
entry in [Chapter 9](#the-gloom) carries three, they are specific to that monster, and they
are always the better choice when one fits. This list is the fallback for when
none of them does.

**The mechanical consequence is not optional.** Every Miss advances the Gloom
clock by 1, or, if the clock is already full, takes 1 Shine from the Star
instead. That happens on every single Miss and it is the whole of the
mechanical move.

What you choose is the *fiction that carries it*. Pick one, and make it change
the situation:

- **One of the Gloom's three moves.** Always the first place to look, because
  they are specific to this monster. Strand someone. Make the distance longer.
  Convince them it was always like this.
- **Separate them.** Distance is the Gloom's favourite weapon.
- **Take something small away.** The phone, the light, the route back.
- **Offer them an easier thing.** Let them out. Let them stop. Make it genuinely
  attractive.
- **Show them what it costs.** Cut to the person who is paying.

Never: hurt a Star permanently, kill anyone, or take a Star's turn away.

**One Miss, one tick, one image.** Do not take the Shine *and* advance the
clock, and do not stack two of the images above onto the same Miss.

## Tone dials

Three, and you should set them out loud in session one.

**How bright?** The default is very. Some tables want the Spectrum uncanny
rather than joyful, colours slightly too loud, glitter slightly too warm. Both
work. Say which you are doing.

**How sincere?** The default is completely. A table that jokes constantly is
usually nervous rather than uninterested; give them one quiet two-person scene
and the register will find itself.

**How much everyday?** The default is about half. Groups who love the Verse can
run whole sessions where the Spectrum appears once, at the end, for ten
enormous minutes. That is a legitimate way to play PRISM and often the best one.

## Common table problems

**Somebody has not spoken in an hour.** The Chord is already working on this, their note is unsounded and the group will need it. But do not wait for the
mechanic. Cut to them. Give them a scene with one other Star and a question.

**The table will not be sincere.** Do not push against the jokes directly. Take
two Stars aside, drop the volume, ask something small and specific, and wait
longer than is comfortable. Sincerity needs a quiet room more than it needs
encouragement.

**They solved it too fast.** They cannot, the clock will not go below 1 before
the Big Finish. Tell them so, and let the last tick be the climax.

**A player wants their Star to die heroically.** No. Offer instead: they are
Dimmed at the moment of victory, and somebody has to come and get them, and
that scene is the last one of the Number. It is better and they will agree
afterwards.

**Nobody is backing anybody.** Usually means the Bonds are thin. Call an
unscheduled quiet scene, put two Stars in it, and ask what actually happened
between them.

## NPCs, and how little they need

People in a PRISM Number do not get stat blocks. They do not roll, they do not
have Traits, and they never oppose a Star mechanically. The Gloom is the only
thing with numbers on it.

What an NPC needs is **one want and one detail**.

> Fen. Works late at the bakery on Tolgate Street. Wants to get home. Keeps
> checking a timetable she already knows.

That is a complete NPC and it will carry a whole session. The want tells you
what they do when the Stars are not looking at them; the detail is what the
players will remember them by, and it is always something small and physical
rather than a fact about their history.

Three kinds are worth having ready:

**The one who is paying.** Every Gloom has somebody bearing the cost. Have them
present, doing an ordinary thing, before the Stars know what the Gloom is. This
is the single highest-value NPC in the session.

**The one who does not notice.** Cheerful, decent, entirely unbothered. *"It's
been like that a while, hasn't it?"* They are not villains and the Stars should
not treat them as such, they are what the Gloom does when it wins slowly.

**The one who tried.** Somebody who noticed earlier, did something, and stopped.
They know things. They are embarrassed. If your Stars need a lead, it is them.

## Pacing a first session

A rough clock for a four-hour evening with a new group.

| Time | What is happening |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Safety tools, then hand out Stars or make them |
| 0:30–1:15 | **Verse.** Ordinary life. The problem arrives at the end |
| 1:15–2:00 | **Chorus.** They try; it is not enough; the clock is revealed |
| 2:00–2:20 | Break. Genuinely, take one |
| 2:20–2:45 | **Bridge.** The quiet scene, the truth, the command word, airtime |
| 2:45–3:45 | **Big Finish.** Turn order, the clock, the Chord |
| 3:45–4:00 | Return to the everyday. The one thing that changed |

The two most common failures are both in the first half: rushing the Verse, and
letting the Chorus become the whole session because nobody has said anything
true yet. If you are at the two-hour mark and no one has morphed, stop
whatever is happening and force a quiet two-person scene.

## Your first three sessions

**Session one.** Use the pregenerated Stars in [Chapter 6](#five-stars-ready-to-play) and The Last Bus in
[Chapter 10](#the-first-number). Do not improvise a Gloom yet. Your entire job is to get comfortable
asking *"what do you say?"* and then waiting.

**Session two.** Same Stars, new Gloom, one from [Chapter 9](#the-gloom), reskinned to your
own town. Now you are practising the conversion procedure with training wheels
still on.

**Session three.** Ask the table: *"what's been allowed to go grey near you?"*
Build the Gloom out of their answer. This is the version of PRISM the book is
actually for, and you should not attempt it first, because it works far better
once everyone trusts the format.

## If you only remember five things

1. **Name the Difficulty before the dice**, and do not move it after.
2. **Every Miss changes the situation**, and the clock tick *is* the move.
3. **Ask for the words**, then wait longer than feels natural.
4. **Reaching a Dimmed Star always works.** No roll, ever, under any
   circumstances.
5. **Come back to the everyday at the end**, and say the one small thing that is
   different now.

Everything else in this chapter is commentary on those five.

## Running the Spectrum without exhausting yourself

The commonest Showrunner complaint about bright settings is that describing
them is tiring. Three habits fix it.

**Three things, then stop.** One colour, one impossible object, one sound. Let
the table build the rest, and they will, immediately, because a maximalist
setting invites contribution in a way a restrained one does not.

**Hand the description away.** *"Toby, what's over there that shouldn't be?"*
Whatever the player says is now true. This costs you nothing, makes the world
feel inhabited, and is the single best trick in the chapter.

**Keep one ordinary thing in frame.** A bin, a bus stop, a specific cracked
paving slab. The Spectrum only feels strange in proportion to how much of the
everyday is still recognisably inside it. A scene with no anchor stops being
uncanny and becomes wallpaper.

## When a session goes wrong

It will, once. Two recoveries worth having ready.

**Nothing is happening and everyone is being polite.** Cut to the person paying
the cost. Not a fight, not a clue, just the NPC, doing an ordinary thing,
visibly worse off than in the last scene. It works almost every time, because
the table wants a reason and you have just handed them one.

**Somebody is not enjoying themselves and you cannot tell why.** Call the break
early. Ask them, away from the table, what they would like their Star to get to
do. Then put that in the next scene, unsubtly. PRISM does not punish you for
being obvious about giving a player what they asked for.

## One last thing

You will be tempted, somewhere around session three, to introduce a Gloom with
a plan. Something that schemes, that has been watching the Stars, that turns
out to have arranged the last two Numbers.

Do not.

The moment a Gloom has intentions it becomes a villain, and a villain makes
this a game about defeating someone. PRISM is a game about *noticing*, about
five people deciding on purpose to care about a thing that was being allowed to
slide. The antagonist has to stay a shape rather than a mind, because the
enemy in this game was never a person. It was the ordinary, forgivable,
universal habit of assuming somebody else had it covered.

Keep the monsters mindless. Put all the intention on the side of the table.
