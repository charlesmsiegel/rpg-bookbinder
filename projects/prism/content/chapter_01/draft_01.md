# Welcome to the Show

## What you do in this game

Somewhere near you, something good is being allowed to go grey.

The night bus that used to run past the chip shop. The library that opens
three days a week now, then two. The friend who stopped answering, and it has
been a month, and everyone assumed somebody else had checked.

In PRISM, you and three or four friends play the people who notice. You cross
into a layer of the world where that greyness has a body — a real body, with a
face and a reach and a name — and you **morph**, and you hit it until it lets
go. You do this on foot, or on a skateboard made of light, or on a robot
unicorn with chrome hooves and a mane like a gradient. All of these are equally
correct.

Then you come home and the bus is running again.

That is the whole game. You are not chosen. You have no bloodline, no secret
academy, no prophecy with your initials on it. You have your friends, a
handful of dice, and the specific stubborn refusal to let a thing you love get
quietly dimmer.

PRISM is bright. It is loud. Nobody dies in it, ever — not the villains, not
you. When you lose a fight you go dim, and you sit there greyed out and
present, and the game's single most important rule is that somebody can always
reach you.

It is also sincere. This book is not going to nudge you in the ribs about how
silly rainbows are. Rainbows are not silly. Bring your whole heart; you will
need it, and so will everyone else at the table.

## The everyday and the Spectrum

There are two layers, and both of them are real.

**The everyday** is the world you actually live in. Not a drab version of it —
just the ordinary one, with its bin collections and its group chats and its
weather. Nothing is hidden here. There is no masquerade to protect and nobody
to keep secrets from. The everyday is simply the world with all its colour
still folded up inside it, the way white light carries every colour at once
without showing you any.

**The Spectrum** is what you get when that light splits.

It is the same places — your street, your school, the launderette on the
corner — rendered in howling airbrushed rainbow. Gradient skies. Chrome that
reflects colours which are not there. Leopard print on things that have no
business wearing leopard print. Dolphins, sometimes, moving through the air
above the ring road as though it were a reef. Glitter behaves like weather over
there, and settles like snow, and nobody finds this strange.

And the neglect has a body.

What is a funding shortfall in the everyday is, in the Spectrum, a thing with
a shape. Something enormous and patient and not really cruel, because cruelty
would require it to want something. It does not want anything. It just keeps
taking, the way a low place keeps collecting water. That is the horror of it,
and also the reason you can beat it: it has no plan. You do.

Crossing over is called **refracting**, and here is the important part — you do
not refract and *then* morph. It is one act. You step sideways out of the
everyday and you arrive already transformed, already glowing, already saying
the thing you say.

What you do over there changes things back here. That is not a metaphor the
book is being coy about. You break the shape, and the route runs again.

## What you need to play

Not much.

- **Three to five people.** One is the Showrunner and runs the world; the rest
  play Stars. Four Stars is the sweet spot.
- **Two six-sided dice.** One pair is genuinely enough, passed around. More is
  nicer.
- **Something to write on.** Character sheets are at the back of this book, and
  you are welcome to photocopy them into oblivion.
- **An evening.** A complete PRISM story — a **Number** — takes one sitting.
  Three to four hours, snacks included.
- **Pencils.** You will be rubbing things out. Bonds change.

That is it. There is no starter set to buy, no map to print, no app.

## Safety and consent at the table

Read this part before you read the rules. It matters more than the rules.

PRISM asks you to be earnest in front of your friends. You will say a
transformation phrase out loud. You will describe what your character looks
like when they are being most themselves. You will, at some point, have your
character admit something true because the mechanics rewarded it. That is a
lot to ask of people, and it is exactly why a game this warm needs *more* care
about consent than a cynical one, not less. Sincerity has no armour on it.

So, before the first Number:

**Lines and veils.** Go round the table. A **line** is something that does not
appear in this game at all — name it, and it is gone, no reasons required and
no follow-up questions. A **veil** is something that can exist but happens off
screen: we cut away, we come back after. Write both lists down where everyone
can see them. Add to them any time, including mid-scene, including without
explaining why.

**A way to stop.** Agree on a signal — an X drawn on a card, a hand raised, the
word "pause". Anyone can use it, including the Showrunner, at any moment. When
it happens, the scene stops immediately and nobody asks what was wrong. You
rewind, adjust, and carry on. Using it is never rude and never a big deal, and
the first time someone uses it the table should thank them, briefly and
genuinely, and then move on.

**Check in at the Encore.** Between Numbers, ask two questions out loud: what
did you love, and is there anything you would rather we did not come back to?
Thirty seconds each. It will change how your next session goes.

**A note on the Gloom.** The things PRISM builds monsters out of are real: a
service being cut, a person being forgotten, someone quietly burning out. Some
of those will be close to somebody's actual life. Ask before you build a Gloom
from a subject, not after. "Is a hospital one okay tonight?" costs nothing.

**Nobody dies.** Worth repeating here, because it changes what is safe to
invest in. Your Star cannot be killed, cannot be permanently maimed, and cannot
be taken away from you. The worst thing that happens is that they go dim and
somebody has to come and get them.

## How to read this book

If you are going to **play a Star**: read this chapter, then Chapter 2 (how
rolling works), then Chapter 3 (how to make someone). That is about forty
minutes and then you are ready. Chapter 6 has five finished Stars if you would
rather just pick one and start tonight — no shame in it, that is what they are
for.

If you are going to **run the game** as Showrunner: read everything, but read
Chapter 8 twice. Chapter 7 teaches you how to turn a real problem in your own
town into a monster, which is the job. Chapter 9 has six ready-made ones for
when you want to skip that step, and Chapter 10 is a complete first Number you
can run cold.

## A note on who gets to shine

Transformation in PRISM is self-expression. That is the whole of it.

Any Star can morph. Any Star can be the one in the flowing coat with the
comet-trail and the phrase that makes people cry, or the one with the working
clockwork and the brass, or the one who is mostly starfield. There is no look,
no colour, no register, and no kind of power that this game considers to belong
to one sort of person.

You will not find this mentioned again. It does not need arguing, and a book
that keeps announcing its own decency is exhausting to read. It is simply how
PRISM works, and every example in here is written that way.

Right. Get your dice.
