# Premise

PRISM is a sugarpop fantasy RPG about transformation — alone and together —
against a spreading dullness called the Gloom. It is set in the modern world
you actually live in, plus one other layer: the Spectrum, where the neglect
eating your town has a body you can hit. On robot unicorns.

## What this book is really about

Most games make you stronger by accumulating things: levels, gear, spells.
PRISM makes you stronger by being **known**. Every rung of its escalation
ladder — extra dice, transformation, the Combined Form — is gated behind other
people choosing to back you at a cost to themselves. The thesis and the math
are the same statement, which is the only way a game about friendship avoids
being a game where friendship is a decoration on top of combat.

## The question it answers

*What would a game look like if helping each other were the optimal play and
not a sacrifice?*

Answered concretely: at Tricky, a Star with +0 backed by a friend succeeds
52.3% of the time; the specialist working alone manages 58.3%. Six points.
Cheap enough that a table will help each other for story reasons, which is
exactly where you want that decision made.

## What it argues that nothing in references/ does

`references/prism/` is PRISM's own canon, so there is no prior art to argue
with. What the book argues against is the *genre's* usual treatment in RPGs:
magical girls and sentai handled as a joke, or as a costume over a combat
game. PRISM plays it straight. Nobody dies. You lose by being dimmed, and you
come back because someone reaches you.

## The shape of a session

One Number: an evening, one problem, one crossing, one monster, one thing in
the real world that is better in the morning.
